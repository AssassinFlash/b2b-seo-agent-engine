## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Extraction (Consolidated)

### 2.1 Core Promise

The article promises to resolve a counter-intent worry: *"If I skip the battery now, am I making a mistake?"* It will show that battery-less solar is viable, explain when it is the smarter move, and give the reader a concrete inverter-selection framework so they do not trap themselves with a non-battery-ready system.

Borrowed from:
- EnergySage — the now-vs-later framing and "storage-ready" action item.
- 饼干哥哥AGI — the contradiction-title reframe (provocation → qualification → lesson).
- JingSourcing — the "platform is safe, but execution varies" conditional promise.

### 2.2 Reader State

The reader arrives after seeing battery-heavy marketing and assumes solar + storage is the default. They fear:
- Missing out on backup power or incentives.
- Buying an inverter that cannot accept a battery later.
- Looking cheap or shortsighted to an installer or spouse.

They want a binary answer (battery or no battery) but actually need a **path decision framework** (Path A / B / C).

### 2.3 Opening Tension

Use a deliberate contradiction: the title/headline challenges the "battery mandatory" assumption, and the opener immediately reframes it — battery-less solar is not a compromise, it is a procurement strategy. Stack proof on top of the provocation within the first 150 words (a real system running without a battery, a spec, or a homeowner scenario).

Borrowed from 饼干哥哥AGI: title says "don't learn n8n," opener reveals the real method.

### 2.4 Angle

Sunriver speaks as a **supply partner / authorized SAJ distributor** that has seen installers and EPCs choose the wrong inverter path and regret it later. The decision point is not "which battery brand?" but "which inverter architecture lets me defer the battery without locking myself out?"

### 2.5 Question Ladder

1. Is solar without a battery actually viable? (Yes — with the right inverter.)
2. Why do so many installers push a battery? (Incentive misalignment, margin, or genuine local need.)
3. What hardware paths let me add a battery later? (R6 on-grid vs. H2 hybrid vs. HS3 all-in-one.)
4. What are the real trade-offs? (Backup, arbitrage, cost, warranty, AHJ rules.)
5. Which path fits my project? (Decision framework.)
6. What question should I ask my installer before signing? (One concrete action item.)

Borrowed from EnergySage + JingSourcing: ladder moves from platform-level reassurance → debunk → operational controls.

### 2.6 Information Progression

Three-act escalation:

1. **Act 1 — Provocation + proof:** Contradictory opener + real system evidence.
2. **Act 2 — Debunk the common path:** Tear down "battery mandatory" and the non-battery-ready inverter trap.
3. **Act 3 — Build the decision framework:** Path A / B / C with specs, trade-offs, and a single action item.

Borrowed from 饼干哥哥AGI: provocation → teardown → demonstration → lesson.

### 2.7 Decision Framework

Give the reader a **Path A / B / C** judgment tool:

- **Path A — String inverter now, retrofit later:** Cheapest upfront; only works if you accept a second install / AC-coupled retrofit later. Risk: higher future labor, possible inverter replacement.
- **Path B — Hybrid inverter now, battery later:** Best future-proofing; H2 runs grid-tied without battery today and accepts SAJ battery tomorrow. Risk: higher upfront inverter cost, but lower total lifecycle cost if battery is added.
- **Path C — All-in-one ESS now:** Simplest; HS3 if backup / arbitrage is required today. Risk: highest upfront capital.

Borrowed from EnergySage: conditional if/then logic.
Borrowed from 饼干哥哥AGI: Bronze/Gold tiered framing.
Borrowed from JingSourcing: numbered red-flag / screening checklist.

### 2.8 Contrast

Contrast is the main engine. Use these pairs:
- Battery now vs. battery later vs. never.
- String vs. hybrid vs. all-in-one.
- AC-coupled retrofit vs. DC-coupled / battery-ready hybrid.
- Homeowner gadget expectation vs. B2B project procurement reality.
- Installer recommendation vs. hardware lifecycle reality.

Borrowed from all three benchmarks; EnergySage provides the now/later mechanical contrast, JingSourcing provides the B2C-vs-B2B contrast.

### 2.9 Specificity

Concrete anchors must include:
- Named SAJ inverter series (R6, H2, HS3) with their actual compatibility claims.
- Voltage / capacity expansion thresholds for future batteries.
- Warranty and transfer terms (with "as of 2026 / confirm with installer" disclaimer).
- Realistic homeowner scenarios with location-agnostic numbers (e.g., "a 10 kW array in a net-metering market").
- AHJ / utility context where relevant.

Borrowed from EnergySage + JingSourcing: named programs, parameter thresholds, and scenario specifics.

### 2.10 Information Density

Every paragraph advances one of: argument, proof, debunk, framework, or action. Compress the whole article promise into a 3–5 bullet "Key takeaways" preview at the top. No generic "solar is the future" paragraphs.

Borrowed from EnergySage: Key takeaways preview.
Borrowed from JingSourcing: no generic background paragraphs.

### 2.11 Cognitive Load

Manage complexity through:
- A top-of-article Path A/B/C map.
- Short H2/H3 hierarchy with a section every 150–300 words.
- Bullet lists for parallel trade-offs.
- Numbered steps for decision sequence.
- One comparison table for the three paths.

Borrowed from JingSourcing + EnergySage: TOC, bullets, numbered prevention steps.

### 2.12 Rhythm

Cadence alternates between:
- Short punchy provocations (woshipm-style).
- Explanatory diagnostic paragraphs (JingSourcing-style).
- Data/spec-heavy sections (EnergySage-style).
- Bullet catalogs and comparison tables.
- A reflective but punchy conclusion.

Intentional repetition: the "battery-ready" concept returns at the opener, the framework, and the closing action item. This is `intentional-rhythm`, not `house-style`.

### 2.13 Proof Strategy

Credibility comes from:
- Product-level specificity (SAJ specs, compatibility matrices).
- Anonymized project scenarios (not "our clients" unless attributed).
- Honest caveats (e.g., no backup during outage, future battery prices uncertain).
- Freshness anchors ("as of 2026").
- External data with source, year, region, scope.

No self-praise. Sunriver does not say "we are the best"; it says "here is how the hardware actually behaves."

### 2.14 Human Texture

The voice must sound like someone who has seen projects go sideways:
- "The installer said the string inverter was fine — until the homeowner asked about adding a battery two years later."
- "That is the retrofit trap in plain English."
- "If your quote assumes a battery today, the math changes whether you actually need backup."

Borrowed from JingSourcing: practitioner fatigue and diagnostic plain-spokenness.
Borrowed from 饼干哥哥AGI: colloquial emotional markers in moderation.

### 2.15 Section Function

Borrowable section jobs (NOT final H2s). Each job should be paired with the `Why → How → Verify` paragraph contract (§4.1) and an assigned callout type where relevant.

1. **Contradiction hook** — title reframe + proof in first 150 words. *Why:* reader assumes battery is mandatory. *How:* reframe + proof. *Verify:* reader understands battery-less solar is a strategy.
2. **Key takeaways preview** — 3–5 bullets that give the whole answer away. *Why:* skimmers and AIO. *How:* bullet list. *Verify:* reader can state Path A/B/C.
3. **Need / path qualification** — force the reader to choose a hardware path before discussing battery timing. *Why:* prevents one-size-fits-all advice. *How:* 2–3 qualifying questions. *Verify:* reader knows which path they are on.
4. **Myth debunk** — "battery mandatory" pressure and the non-battery-ready trap. *Why:* counters the most common misinformation. *How:* wrong-way teardown + right-way spec. *Verify:* reader can explain the retrofit trap. *[WARNING] possible.*
5. **Path framework** — Path A / B / C comparison with specs and trade-offs. *Why:* the core decision tool. *How:* comparison table + scenario. *Verify:* reader can pick a path for their project.
6. **Red-flag catalog** — installer-quote warning signs. *Why:* defensive competence. *How:* numbered list of warning signs. *Verify:* reader can screen a quote. *[IMPORTANT] or [PRO TIP] possible.*
7. **Single action item** — the one question to ask before signing. *Why:* conversion to action. *How:* copy-paste question. *Verify:* reader knows what to ask.
8. **Lesson-pivot close** — reframe from "no battery" to "right battery-ready hardware path." *Why:* memorability and next-step routing. *How:* punchy reframe + launchpad links. *Verify:* reader remembers the reframe phrase.
9. **Soft product CTA** — route to R6 / H2 / HS3 pages or contact. *Why:* move reader to technical review. *How:* path-specific link. *Verify:* reader clicks the relevant product page.

## 4. Writer's Playbook (for `/article-skeleton`)

### 4.1 Paragraph contract: Why → How → Verify

Every section that teaches a decision or action should follow this three-part shape:

1. **Why (Context):** 1–2 sentences explaining why this section matters and what risk it addresses.
2. **How (Action):** The core instruction, comparison, or framework, using imperative or direct language.
3. **Verify (Feedback):** The expected outcome, a check the reader can perform, or a concrete sign that the decision is correct.

Borrowed from S4 / Ahrefs + DigitalOcean + iFixit. Calibrate to Sunriver's contrarian voice: the "Why" can be a provocation, not a neutral setup.

### 4.2 Callout taxonomy

Use three callout types consistently:

- **`[WARNING]`** — Safety, warranty, or compliance caveats that could cost the reader money or cause rework. Example: *"[WARNING] A string inverter without AC-coupled battery support will require an inverter swap if you later want DC-coupled battery backup."*
- **`[IMPORTANT]`** — Technical details essential for success that are easy to miss. Example: *"[IMPORTANT] The SAJ H2 can run grid-tied without a battery, but battery expansion voltage and capacity thresholds must match the future battery module."*
- **`[PRO TIP]`** — Practitioner advice framed as experience, not self-praise. Example: *"[PRO TIP] From what Sunriver sees in the field, the most expensive retrofit is not the battery — it is replacing an inverter that was never battery-ready."*

### 4.3 Visual contract

- **One concept, one diagram:** Anytime the article introduces a new decision framework (e.g., Path A/B/C, AC vs. DC coupling), pair it with a diagram or comparison table.
- **Product images:** Use Sunriver product photography for R6, H2, and HS3 when named.
- **Annotated screenshots:** Only if showing the eSAJ app or a config screen; otherwise prefer diagrams and tables.
- **Comparison table:** One mandatory table comparing Path A, Path B, and Path C across cost now, future battery cost, backup today, and inverter replacement risk.
- **Image plan handoff:** `/article-enrich` should turn this contract into `image-plan.md` with alt text tied to the article's decision framework.

### 4.4 Expectation-setting opener block

Immediately after the contradiction hook, include a short block that sets expectations:

- **What you will learn:** One sentence summarizing the decision framework.
- **What you should already know:** Basic solar terminology (inverter, battery, grid-tied, hybrid).
- **What you will be able to do:** Choose Path A, B, or C and ask one hard question before signing.

### 4.5 Launchpad conclusion block

The conclusion must do more than summarize. It should:

1. Restate the core reframe in one punchy sentence.
2. Offer a single action item (the installer question).
3. Route the reader to the next most relevant Sunriver resource based on their likely path:
   - Path A → `/product/r6-15k-25k-three-phase-on-grid-inverter/`
   - Path B → `/product/h2-3k-6k-single-phase-hybrid-inverter/`
   - Path C → `/product/hs3-3k-6k-single-phase-all-in-one-energy-storage-system/`
